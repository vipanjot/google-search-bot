# [[Active inference]]

Ara Eve Leo Sal

Active inference is a theoretical framework in [cognitive science](</page/Cognitive_science>), [neuroscience](</page/Neuroscience>), and artificial intelligence that posits biological and artificial agents minimize prediction errors by performing [Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_inference>) to perceive, plan, and act within their environments, building on the [[free energy principle]] to enable [adaptive behavior](</page/Adaptive_behavior>) through active engagement in perception-action loops.[1][2] Pioneered by [Karl Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>) at University College London since the early 2010s, this approach distinguishes itself from passive inference models by emphasizing how agents actively sample their surroundings to reduce uncertainty and free energy, thereby fostering goal-directed and habitual behaviors.[2][3] The framework is deeply rooted in the **free energy principle** , which suggests that living systems maintain their integrity by minimizing variational free energyâa bound on surprise or prediction errorâthrough [generative models](</page/Generative_model>) that predict sensory inputs.[1] This principle underpins active inference's account of learning and decision-making, where agents update internal models via perceptual inference (updating [beliefs about the world](</page/Bayesian_approaches_to_brain_function>)) and active inference (selecting actions that confirm those beliefs or resolve uncertainty).[3] In [neuroscience](</page/Neuroscience>), active inference has been applied to model brain functions, such as [cortical hierarchies](</page/Cerebral_cortex>) and neural dynamics, providing insights into phenomena like attention, [motor control](</page/Motor_control>), and even [psychiatric disorders](</page/Mental_disorder>) through the lens of dysregulated prediction error minimization.[4][2] In artificial intelligence, active inference offers a biologically inspired alternative to traditional reinforcement learning by enabling autonomous systems to reason, plan, and learn in uncertain environments without explicit reward signals, potentially advancing fields like roboticsâwhere it supports unified perception, action, and learning with causal reasoning for transparent decision-makingâand brain-computer interfaces.[5][2][6] Key affiliations include the Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, where Friston and collaborators have driven much of the foundational work, leading to influential publications that integrate active inference with predictive processing theories.[1] Notable achievements encompass simulations of sentient behavior, empirical validations in behavioral experiments, and extensions to multi-agent systems, highlighting its potential for scalable AI that mimics natural intelligence.[3][4]

## History and Development

### Origins in Predictive Processing

[Predictive processing](</page/Predictive_processing>) emerged as a foundational theory in [cognitive science](</page/Cognitive_science>) and [neuroscience](</page/Neuroscience>), positing that the brain functions as a predictive machine that generates [top-down expectations](</page/Perception>) about [sensory inputs](</page/Sense>) to minimize discrepancies with actual sensations.[7] This framework traces its conceptual roots to [Hermann von Helmholtz](</page/Hermann_von_Helmholtz>)'s 1867 notion of "[unconscious inference](</page/Unconscious_inference>)," where [perception](</page/Perception>) is described as an automatic, [inferential process](</page/Unconscious_inference>) by which the brain interprets ambiguous sensory data based on [prior knowledge](</page/Prior_probability>) and assumptions, rather than passively receiving stimuli.[8] Helmholtz's ideas laid the groundwork for viewing perception not as [direct representation](</page/Direct_and_indirect_realism>) but as a form of [hypothesis formation](</page/Unconscious_inference>), influencing later developments in how organisms actively construct their understanding of the environment.[7] The revival of these [Helmholtzian principles](</page/Unconscious_inference>) occurred in the late 20th century, particularly through computational models in the 1990s that formalized predictive processing in neural terms. A seminal contribution came from Rajesh P. N. Rao and Dana H. Ballard in their 1999 paper, which proposed a hierarchical predictive coding model for [visual processing](</page/Visual_processing>) in the [cortex](</page/Cerebral_cortex>), where higher-level areas send predictive signals downward to anticipate and suppress expected activity in lower areas, thereby enhancing efficiency in handling sensory surprises.[9] This model shifted emphasis from [bottom-up feature detection](</page/Visual_processing>) to a bidirectional process of prediction and error correction, bridging [early philosophical ideas](</page/Unconscious_inference>) with modern neuroscience.[9] Another key milestone in this lineage was [Richard L. Gregory](</page/Richard_Gregory>)'s 1980 work on [perceptions as hypotheses](</page/Richard_Gregory>), which emphasized hypothesis testing in [visual perception](</page/Visual_perception>) as an active, exploratory process akin to scientific inference, where the brain generates and refines predictions to resolve [perceptual ambiguities](</page/Ambiguous_image>).[10] Gregory's framework highlighted the role of [prior knowledge](</page/Perception>) and [top-down influences](</page/Perception>) in overcoming sensory limitations, influencing the transition in [[active inference]] from passive sensory models to dynamic, action-oriented ones that engage the agent in testing perceptual hypotheses through behavior.[11] Active inference originated as an extension of predictive processing in Karl Friston's 2010 paper, "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?," which synthesized these ideas with variational Bayesian methods to explain how agents minimize prediction errors not only through perception but also through action.[12] This synthesis positioned active inference as a normative framework for understanding adaptive behavior under the free-energy principle, marking a pivotal conceptual evolution from static predictive models to integrated perception-action cycles.[12]

### Key Contributors and Milestones

[Karl Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>), a Professor of Imaging Neuroscience at University College London and affiliated with the Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, is recognized as the primary developer of [[active inference]] since the early 2010s, building on his foundational work in theoretical neurobiology.[13] His contributions have centered on formalizing active inference as a process for minimizing [variational free energy](</page/Variational_Bayesian_methods>) in [biological systems](</page/Biological_system>), with core papers garnering thousands of citations, far exceeding 1,000 by 2023 as evidenced by his [Google Scholar](</page/Google_Scholar>) profile.[14] [Anil Seth](</page/Anil_Seth>), a professor at the [University of Sussex](</page/University_of_Sussex>), has been a key contributor in applying active inference to the study of [consciousness](</page/Consciousness>), particularly through frameworks that integrate predictive processing with interoceptive inference to explain [subjective experience](</page/Subjective_character_of_experience>).[15] Collaborations such as Hobson and [Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>)'s work on dreaming have extended active inference to model consciousness during sleep states, as detailed in their 2014 paper exploring virtual reality generation in the brain via [inference mechanisms](</page/Bayesian_approaches_to_brain_function>).[16] A pivotal milestone occurred in 2010 with Friston's publication of "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" in _Nature Reviews Neuroscience_ , which laid the groundwork for active inference by proposing that biological agents minimize free energy to maintain [homeostasis](</page/Homeostasis>).[12] This was followed in 2011 by the development of the first computational models of active inference, exemplified in the paper "Action understanding and active inference," which demonstrated [neuronal simulations](</page/Brain_simulation>) for encoding motor intentions and behavior prescription.[17] The formalization of [active inference](</page/active_inference>) advanced in 2012 with [Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>)'s paper "Dopamine, affordance and active inference" in _[PLoS Computational Biology](</page/PLoS_Computational_Biology>)_ , linking [dopaminergic systems](</page/dopaminergic_systems>) to [hierarchical Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_hierarchical_modeling>) in [action selection](</page/action_selection>).[18] Empirical validations in [neuroscience](</page/Neuroscience>) emerged around 2015, as seen in "Active inference and epistemic value," which provided a formal treatment of choice behavior under active inference, supporting its applicability to real-world decision-making processes.[19] Extensions to social inference were developed by 2017, with [Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>) and collaborators exploring active inference in social contexts through [models of communication](</page/Models_of_communication>) and [normative behavior](</page/Social_norm>), building on [Bayesian principles](</page/Bayesian_inference>) for [interpersonal predictions](</page/Social_cognition>). In the [2020s](</page/2020s>), active inference saw integrations with artificial intelligence, including theoretical advancements that influenced autonomous systems design.[20]

### Evolution and Recent Advances

[[Active inference]] has evolved significantly in the 2020s, transitioning from its primary roots in neuroscience toward broader integrations with artificial intelligence, particularly in reinforcement learning frameworks. This shift is exemplified by 2021 publications that explore how active inference can enhance decision-making in AI agents by incorporating predictive processing to minimize free energy in dynamic environments, as demonstrated in the NeurIPS paper on Contrastive Active Inference, which proposes a method to reduce model complexity while improving exploratory behaviors in uncertain settings.[21] These integrations address limitations in traditional reinforcement learning by enabling agents to actively infer hidden states and adapt without relying solely on reward signals, marking a key evolution toward more autonomous systems.[22] Recent advances in 2023 have focused on developing scalable algorithms for real-world robotics applications, leveraging active inference to enable [efficient control](</page/Robot_control>) in complex, uncertain environments. For instance, research has shown that deep kinematic inference under active inference principles allows robots to generate [goal-directed movements](</page/Motor_control>) by predicting sensory outcomes, achieving scalable performance in tasks like manipulation without extensive [training data](</page/Supervised_learning>).[23] This approach has been highlighted as a potential revolution in robotics, offering algorithmic improvements for safer and more robust AI systems through epistemic value maximization in graphical models.[24] Concurrently, empirical studies using fMRI have validated active inference in [human decision-making](</page/Decision_theory>), particularly through investigations of precision weighting, where 2023 research revealed that the [orbitofrontal cortex](</page/Orbitofrontal_cortex>) transiently signals unexpected outcomes to sensory areas, aligning with precision-weighted prediction error minimization during value-based choices.[25] In [psychiatry](</page/psychiatry>), 2024 reviews have underscored active inference's growing role in explaining disorders through aberrant precision weighting and inference processes, providing a unified framework for symptoms like [apathy](</page/apathy>) and [conceptual disorganization](</page/Thought_disorder>) in schizophrenia.[26] These reviews highlight progress in applying active inference to therapeutic contexts, such as modeling [optimism bias](</page/Optimism_bias>) as high-precision priors favoring positive outcomes, which informs interventions for [mood disorders](</page/Mood_disorder>).[27] Emerging discussions in the literature have also explored hypothetical quantum extensions to active inference, proposing [quantum models](</page/Quantum_cognition>) that naturally implement [variational free energy minimization](</page/variational_free_energy_minimization>) for conscious behavior, potentially bridging classical and [quantum cognition](</page/Quantum_cognition>) in sentient systems.[28] A notable event in this evolution was the 2021 [NeurIPS](</page/NeurIPS>) contributions on active inference frameworks, including papers that fostered interdisciplinary dialogue on integrating these principles with [machine learning](</page/Outline_of_machine_learning>).[21] This progression reflects active inference's maturation, with [sensorimotor coupling](</page/Sensory-motor_coupling>) evolving as a core mechanism for adaptive inference in both biological and [artificial agents](</page/artificial_agents>).[29]

## Theoretical Foundations

### Bayesian Inference Basics

[Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_inference>) is a statistical method for updating the [probability](</page/Probability>) of a [hypothesis](</page/Statistical_hypothesis_test>) based on new evidence, forming the probabilistic foundation of [[active inference]] frameworks in [cognitive science](</page/Cognitive_science>). Originating from Thomas Bayes' posthumously published 1763 essay "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances," it experienced a modern revival in the [1990s](</page/1990s>) through computational neuroscience, where it became central to modeling uncertain environments in biological and artificial systems.[30] This approach allows agents to represent beliefs about the world as [probability distributions](</page/Probability_distribution>), enabling [rational decision-making](</page/Decision_theory>) under uncertainty. At its core, [Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_inference>) relies on Bayes' theorem, which mathematically describes how to compute the [posterior probability](</page/Posterior_probability>) of a hypothesis given evidence. The theorem is derived from the definition of conditional probability: the [joint probability](</page/Joint_probability_distribution>) P(H, E) equals P(H|E) P(E) or P(E|H) P(H), leading to P(H|E) = [P(E|H) P(H)] / P(E), where H represents the hypothesis, E the evidence, P(H) the [prior probability](</page/Prior_probability>), P(E|H) the [likelihood](</page/Likelihood_function>), and P(E) the [marginal likelihood](</page/Marginal_likelihood>) or evidence. For clarity, this can be expressed as:

A simple example illustrates this: suppose an agent has a [prior belief](</page/Prior_probability>) [P(H)](</page/Prior_probability>) = 0.5 that a [coin is fair](</page/Fair_coin>) (hypothesis H), and observes evidence E of 3 heads in 5 flips with [likelihood P(E|H)](</page/Likelihood_function>) = 0.3125 (binomial probability); the evidence [P(E)](</page/Bayesian_probability>) is computed by [marginalizing over hypotheses](</page/Marginal_distribution>), yielding a [posterior P(H|E)](</page/Posterior_probability>) that updates the belief based on the [marginal likelihood](</page/Marginal_likelihood>), potentially downward if an alternative [biased hypothesis](</page/Checking_whether_a_coin_is_fair>) fits the data better. In this framework, the [prior P(H)](</page/Prior_probability>) encodes initial beliefs derived from past experiences or [innate assumptions](</page/Innatism>), the likelihood P(E|H) measures how well the hypothesis predicts the observed data, and the posterior P(H|E) integrates both to form [updated beliefs](</page/Bayesian_epistemology>), which can then serve as new [priors](</page/Prior_probability>) for future [inferences](</page/Statistical_inference>). In complex models relevant to active inference, exact [Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_inference>) becomes computationally intractable due to high-dimensional integrals for P(E), necessitating approximations such as [variational Bayesian methods](</page/Variational_Bayesian_methods>). These methods optimize a lower bound on the [log-evidence](</page/Marginal_likelihood>) ([variational free energy](</page/Variational_Bayesian_methods>)) to approximate the [true posterior](</page/Posterior_probability>), enabling scalable inference in [hierarchical generative models](</page/Bayesian_hierarchical_modeling>) where agents simulate sensory data from internal states. [Generative models](</page/Generative_model>) in this context are probabilistic representations that generate expected observations from [latent causes](</page/Latent_variable_model>), allowing active inference agents to infer [hidden states](</page/Hidden_Markov_model>) by inverting these models through [Bayesian updates](</page/Bayesian_inference>), thus linking [perception](</page/Perception>) to [probabilistic prediction](</page/Probabilistic_forecasting>). Prediction error minimization emerges as a practical Bayesian mechanism for refining these inferences iteratively.

### [[Free Energy Principle]]

The [free energy principle](</page/free_energy_principle>), introduced by [Karl Friston](</page/Karl_J._Friston>) in 2006, posits that biological agents maintain their integrity by minimizing variational [free energy](</page/free_energy>), a quantity that serves as an upper bound on [surprise](</page/Information_content>), defined as the [negative log probability](</page/Log_probability>) of sensory data.[31][32] This principle provides a unified framework for understanding [perception](</page/Perception>), action, and learning as processes that approximate [Bayesian inference](</page/Bayesian_inference>) under uncertainty, ensuring agents avoid unexpectedly low-likelihood states that could threaten their viability.[33] By optimizing free energy, agents effectively perform [approximate Bayesian updates](</page/Approximate_Bayesian_computation>) on their [generative models](</page/Generative_model>) of the world, balancing [model evidence](</page/Bayesian_inference>) with predictive accuracy. where $ \KL[\cdot \parallel \cdot] $ denotes the [Kullback-Leibler divergence](</page/KullbackâLeibler_divergence>) between the [approximate posterior](</page/Variational_Bayesian_methods>) $ Q(\mu) $ and the [true posterior](</page/Posterior_probability>) $ P(\mu|s) $, with $ \mu $ representing internal model parameters and $ s $ denoting sensory states; the term $ -\ln P(s) $ captures surprise.[34] This formulation arises from [variational inference techniques](</page/Variational_Bayesian_methods>), where minimizing $ F $ minimizes the divergence between the approximate and true posteriors while maximizing the [evidence lower bound (ELBO)](</page/Evidence_lower_bound>), thereby bounding surprise since $ F \geq -\ln P(s) $, with equality holding for the [exact posterior](</page/Posterior_probability>).[35] The derivation underscores that free energy minimization is equivalent to Bayesian model selection, as it selects [generative models](</page/Generative_model>) that best explain sensory data while penalizing complexity.[36] Central to the principle are [Markov blankets](</page/Markov_blanket>), which delineate the boundaries of an agent by separating its internal states from external environmental influences, enabling [conditional independence](</page/Conditional_independence>) in [inference processes](</page/Bayesian_inference>).[37] These blankets consist of [sensory and active states](</page/Markov_blanket>) that interface with the environment, allowing the agent to infer [hidden causes](</page/Bayesian_inference>) while shielding its internal dynamics. ### Predictive Coding Framework

[Predictive coding](</page/Predictive_coding>) serves as a foundational neural mechanism within [[active inference]], modeling the [brain](</page/Human_brain>) as a hierarchical system of [prediction machines](</page/prediction_machines>) that generate top-down predictions about sensory inputs and compute [bottom-up prediction errors](</page/Mismatch_negativity>) to refine those predictions.[39] In this framework, [cortical areas](</page/Cerebral_cortex>) are organized into levels of increasing abstraction, where higher levels send [predictive signals](</page/predictive_signals>) downward to lower levels, anticipating sensory data, while discrepanciesâknown as prediction errorsâare propagated upward to update the internal models at each level.[40] This hierarchical structure enables efficient processing by minimizing surprise through [continuous inference](</page/Bayesian_approaches_to_brain_function>), aligning with the broader objective of minimizing [free energy](</page/free_energy>) in [active inference formulations](</page/active_inference_formulations>).[41] A seminal implementation of this framework is the neural network model proposed by Rao and Ballard in 1999, which incorporates distinct error units and prediction units across hierarchical layers to simulate visual processing in the cortex.[40] In their model, prediction units at each level generate expected sensory states based on higher-level representations, while error units calculate the mismatch between actual and predicted inputs, with these errors weighted by precision estimates to prioritize reliable signals over noisy ones.[42] Precision weighting thus modulates the influence of prediction errors, allowing the system to adapt to varying levels of sensory uncertainty.[39] At the core of predictive coding is the computation of prediction errors, formally expressed as the difference between observed sensory input and the predicted mean:

## Core Mechanisms

### Prediction Error Minimization

Prediction error minimization is a core process in [[active inference]], where agents reduce discrepancies between their internal predictions and sensory observations to maintain accurate models of the world. This minimization can occur through two primary strategies: perceptual inference, which involves updating internal beliefs to better match incoming sensory data, and active inference, which entails selecting actions that alter the environment to align it with those beliefs. Perceptual inference achieves this by revising generative models via Bayesian updating, effectively minimizing surprise or free energy by adjusting expectations, while active inference does so by resampling sensory inputs through motor actions, thereby resolving prediction errors without solely relying on belief revision.[45] A key aspect of this process is the role of precision weighting, defined as the inverse variance of prediction errors, which determines the influence of sensory signals on belief updating. Precision acts as a gating mechanism, amplifying reliable (high-precision) prediction errors to drive stronger adjustments in the agent's model, while down-weighting noisy or unreliable ones to prevent overfitting to spurious data. This precision optimization ensures that minimization is adaptive, balancing the reliability of sensory evidence against prior beliefs in a Bayesian manner.[46] In active inference, action selection is guided by minimizing expected free energy, a quantity that encapsulates both pragmatic value (achieving desired outcomes) and epistemic value (reducing uncertainty about the environment). The expected free energy for a policy is formulated as the sum of risk (divergence from preferred outcomes) and ambiguity (expected surprise), with epistemic value promoting information gain to resolve uncertainty, thus addressing the exploration-exploitation trade-off. By selecting policies that minimize this expected free energy, agents balance exploiting known rewarding actions with exploring novel ones to refine their world model.[3] The minimization process is mathematically described by gradient descent on the free energy functional, where beliefs Î¼\muÎ¼ evolve according to the differential equation:

In contrast, active minimization integrates action into the loop, enabling agents to test hypotheses and gather informative data, which is particularly advantageous for autonomous systems where passive strategies may fail to resolve deep uncertainties. This active approach, central to Friston's 2013 formulation, distinguishes active inference by emphasizing embodied interaction as a fundamental minimization strategy.[47]

### Sensorimotor Coupling

In [[active inference]], [sensorimotor coupling](</page/Sensory-motor_coupling>) refers to the integrated processes through which agents engage in [action-perception cycles](</page/Embodied_cognition>), where sensory inputs and motor outputs are tightly intertwined to minimize prediction errors in real-time interactions with the environment.[50] This coupling is facilitated by mechanisms such as [efference copies](</page/Efference_copy>), which provide predictive signals about the sensory consequences of intended actions, allowing the agent to anticipate and adjust to self-generated sensory changes without mistaking them for external perturbations.[51] Such cycles enable autonomous behavior in [self-organizing systems](</page/Self-organization>), where agents maintain their integrity by actively sampling the environment to resolve uncertainties, embodying a form of epistemic foraging that promotes [adaptive homeostasis](</page/Homeostasis>).[52] This framework draws conceptual links to [enactive cognition](</page/Enactivism>), particularly through the notion of [sensorimotor contingencies](</page/Enactivism#sensorimotor-contingencies-and-perception>) as articulated by [Alva NoÃ«](</page/Alva_NoÃ«>) in his 2004 work _Action in Perception_ , where perception is understood as an active exploration of lawful relationships between movements and sensory feedback.[53] In active inference, these contingencies are modeled generatively, allowing agents to infer hidden states of the world by exploiting predictable sensorimotor dependencies, thus extending NoÃ«'s ideas into a Bayesian mechanistic account.[54] For instance, in [visuomotor control tasks](</page/Visual_servoing>), active inference has been applied to robotic systems where a simulated PR2 robot's 7-degree-of-freedom arm adjusts its movements to align visual predictions with ### Clinical Implications

[[Active inference]] has significant implications for understanding [psychiatric disorders](</page/Mental_disorder>), particularly through the lens of aberrant precision weighting in predictive processing. In schizophrenia, hallucinations are conceptualized as false positive inferences arising from overly precise [priors](</page/Prior_probability>) that overpower sensory evidence, leading to the perception of internal predictions as external realities.[70] This aberrant precision is thought to disrupt the balance between top-down expectations and bottom-up sensory inputs, contributing to symptoms like [auditory hallucinations](</page/Auditory_hallucination>) where patients experience voices as [veridical](</page/Direct_and_indirect_realism>) despite lacking external corroboration.[71] Empirical support for this comes from studies showing that individuals with schizophrenia exhibit reduced evidence accumulation in [decision-making tasks](</page/Decision-making>), consistent with heightened reliance on imprecise sensory signals.[72] In autism spectrum disorder, active inference accounts propose that symptoms stem from failures in sensory attenuation, where the precision of prediction errors from self-generated actions is not properly downregulated. This results in heightened sensitivity to sensory inputs, as exteroceptive and proprioceptive signals fail to align with internal models, leading to overwhelming perceptual experiences and challenges in social inference.[73] For instance, individuals with autism may exhibit atypical gaze behaviors in virtual reality tasks, reflecting disrupted active sampling to minimize surprise, which underscores the role of attenuated autonomic predictions in the disorder.[74] Such mechanisms highlight how adaptation processes, as modeled in active inference, become disrupted, impairing flexible responses to environmental contingencies in clinical contexts.[75] Models of depression within active inference frame the disorder as arising from "stuck" or inflexible policies, where agents become locked into maladaptive generative models that prioritize avoidance over exploration, perpetuating low mood and anhedonia. A 2025 conceptualization aligns depression with a failure in allostatic regulation, where imprecise interoceptive inferences lead to persistent uncertainty about energy regulation and future prospects, trapping individuals in cycles of rumination and withdrawal.[76] Therapeutic applications build on this by incorporating precision-weighted cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which targets the adjustment of belief precisions to enhance inference flexibility; a 2025 article discusses this approach and its potential to improve symptom relief by recalibrating patients' weighting of maladaptive priors during therapy sessions.[77] [Empirical validation](</page/Empirical_research>) includes 2021 studies using [EEG](</page/Electroencephalography>) to simulate how active inference models can predict neural responses related to preparatory attention, with potential applications to clinical populations such as altered signals in [psychotic disorders](</page/Psychosis>), supporting the framework's utility in neuroimaging biomarkers for treatment monitoring.[78] Post-2020 integrations with [psychedelics](</page/List_of_psychedelic_drugs>) further extend these implications, positing that substances like psilocybin enhance inference flexibility by reducing the precision of rigid self-models, potentially alleviating symptoms in depression and anxiety through induced [ego-dissolution](</page/Ego_death>) and reopened policy spaces.[79] Integrations with psychedelic-assisted therapy for treatment-resistant depression illustrate how this flexibility may restore adaptive active inference, enabling patients to update entrenched negative beliefs more effectively.

## Criticisms and Future Directions

### Major Critiques

[[Active inference]], while influential in cognitive science and neuroscience, has faced several theoretical and philosophical critiques that question its foundational assumptions and explanatory power. One prominent criticism concerns the circularity inherent in the concept of self-evidencing, where the framework posits that agents infer their own existence and boundaries through predictive processes that simultaneously rely on those boundaries for accurate predictions. This circularity, argued to undermine the coherence of the theory, risks tautological reasoning by presupposing the very structures it aims to explain.[109] Critics have also pointed to an over-reliance on the [[free energy principle]] as a universal explanatory mechanism, portraying it as a panacea that stretches beyond its empirical warrant to encompass diverse phenomena from perception to decision-making without sufficient differentiation. This view suggests that the principle's broad applicability may mask underlying complexities in biological systems, potentially leading to oversimplification of agent-environment interactions. Such concerns have been raised in discussions emphasizing the need for more nuanced models that avoid treating free energy minimization as an all-encompassing solution.[110] Debates surrounding the realism of Markov blanketsâhypothetical statistical boundaries separating an agent from its environmentâfurther underscore philosophical vulnerabilities in active inference. Critiques from 2020, including those by researchers like Manuel Baltieri and Christopher L. Buckley, argue that Markov blankets may not correspond to tangible biological or physical entities, rendering them more as mathematical artifacts than realistic descriptions of autonomy and individuality. This has implications for the theory's ability to ground claims about genuine agency in living systems.[111] Additionally, the framework has been accused of lacking falsifiability, a cornerstone of scientific theories, as its flexible Bayesian mechanisms can accommodate a wide array of observations without clear criteria for refutation. This issue, debated in philosophical literature, raises questions about whether active inference functions more as a descriptive heuristic than a testable hypothesis.[112] Philosophical responses to these critiques have appeared in 2022 journal articles, addressing issues like agency and self-evidencing while attempting to refine the theory's metaphysical commitments. In relation to broader empirical challenges, some theoretical flaws are seen as intertwined but distinct from validation issues in experimental settings.

### Empirical Challenges

One major empirical challenge in [[active inference]] lies in its scalability to high-dimensional data and complex environments, where computational demands can hinder efficient implementation and testing. For instance, while active inference has shown promise in low-dimensional tasks, extending it to high-dimensional continuous action spaces often faces issues with stability and computational efficiency, limiting its applicability in real-world scenarios like robotics.[113] Similarly, empirical evaluations in multi-armed bandit problems reveal that active inference algorithms may fail to produce efficient long-term behavior in stationary environments, underscoring scalability limitations compared to more established methods.[114] Distinguishing active inference from alternative models, such as reinforcement learning (RL), poses another significant hurdle in experimental validation, as both frameworks can generate similar behavioral outcomes without clear mechanistic differentiation. In controlled experiments, active inference's emphasis on minimizing prediction errors through perception-action loops often overlaps with RL's reward-based optimization, making it difficult to isolate unique signatures of active inference without tailored interventions. This overlap complicates empirical testing, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Calls for causal interventions in active inference research intensified around 2021, as observational data alone proved insufficient for establishing causality in agent-environment interactions. Proponents argued that active interventions, such as targeted perturbations in neural models, are essential to test how agents actively minimize free energy beyond passive inference.[115] This shift toward interventional approaches aims to address gaps in understanding decision-making under uncertainty, as explored in frameworks integrating active inference with causal modeling.[116] A notable gap persists in animal model validations of active inference, with limited empirical support from non-human studies despite theoretical predictions. For example, while computational models simulate rodent navigation under active inference, real-world validations in animal experiments remain sparse, often relying on indirect behavioral proxies rather than direct neural measurements. Examples of failed predictions in pain perception studies further illustrate empirical challenges, where active inference models sometimes mismatch observed neural responses. In experiments on expectation and pain, prediction error signals in the anterior insula did not align with aversiveness as anticipated, leading to critiques of the framework's precision weighting in sensory processing.[117] Additionally, some studies on pain controllability revealing discrepancies between model predictions and empirical outcomes. Such instances, including failed replications of pain-related expectation effects, underscore the difficulties in validating active inference in sensory domains.[118] Clinical implications serve as potential testing grounds for these challenges, where active inference could inform interventions in pain disorders but requires stronger empirical backing to move beyond simulation.

### Emerging Research Areas

Emerging research in [[active inference]] is increasingly exploring interdisciplinary frontiers that extend the framework beyond traditional cognitive and neural applications. One promising area involves hypothetical proposals for quantum active inference, which seek to integrate quantum mechanics into the [[free energy principle]] to model complex decision-making processes at a fundamental level. For instance, researchers have proposed quantum models that naturally implement the path integral required for real-time planning and control in active inference systems, suggesting potential enhancements in handling uncertainty and non-classical phenomena in agent behavior.[28] These 2025 proposals, such as those outlined in formulations connecting classical and quantum active inference, aim to provide biologically plausible mechanisms for conscious processing and could revolutionize simulations of sentient agents.[119] Social and multi-agent extensions represent another key emerging direction, where active inference is adapted to model interactions among multiple agents, fostering collective behaviors and strategic decision-making. Recent work has introduced empathy mechanisms within active inference frameworks to enable artificial agents to exhibit socially appropriate behaviors, addressing challenges in defining and implementing social norms across diverse contexts.[120] Factorized active inference approaches have been developed for strategic multi-agent interactions, grounding decision-making in Bayesian inference while incorporating bounded rationality to better simulate realistic social dynamics.[121] Additionally, studies on shared protentions in multi-agent active inference explore how temporal structures emerge in collective systems, bridging phenomenology with computational models to enhance coordination in group settings.[122] Integrations with quantum cognition are gaining traction as a specific conceptual advancement, linking active inference's predictive processing to quantum-inspired models of human reasoning. This involves connecting the free energy minimization principle to quantum cognition theories, which account for phenomena like contextuality and interference in decision-making that classical Bayesian models struggle to explain.[123] Such integrations propose that quantum orchestrated objective reduction could synchronize asynchronous neural signals in active inference, offering a mechanistic basis for conscious perception and adaptive inference in cognitive systems.[124] Applications of active inference to climate modeling through adaptive agents are emerging as a novel practical domain, leveraging the framework's strengths in simulating sustainable decision-making under uncertainty. For example, active inference has been applied to model sustainable resource management, where agents adaptively infer environmental states to optimize resource allocation and mitigate depletion, providing a foundation for broader climate adaptation strategies.[125] These agent-based models demonstrate how active inference can support lifelong learning in heterogeneous environments, potentially extending to climate scenarios involving dynamic, unpredictable factors like weather variability.[126] Post-2023 frontiers in active inference increasingly emphasize collective intelligence, building on multi-agent extensions to explore emergent group-level inference in complex systems. This includes ongoing developments in distributed active inference across agents, which evolve generative models to support collective problem-solving and adaptive behaviors in social and ecological contexts. Conference trends in 2024, such as those at the Constellation Connected Enterprise (CCE), highlight active inference's role in enterprise AI, with discussions on ethical implications for scalable, inference-based systems in multi-agent and collective settings.[127] These trends underscore the need for ethical frameworks in AI inference, particularly as active inference advances toward applications in collective intelligence and beyond.[128]
